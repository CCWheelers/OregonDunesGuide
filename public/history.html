The history of
the Oregon Dunes.

Rivers delivered the sand. Wind moved it inland. Native people built enduring cultures around the coast’s rivers, forests, lakes, and ocean. Later generations tried to travel, settle, stabilize, protect, and play in a landscape that never stopped changing.

THE LONG VIEW

Not empty land.
Not a fixed landscape.

The Oregon Dunes are both a living natural system and part of the homelands of the Coos, Lower Umpqua, Siuslaw, and neighboring peoples.

Any honest history begins before federal designations, highways, and recreation maps. It also recognizes that Native communities remain present today, carrying living cultures and continuing relationships with these lands and waters.

TIME IMMEMORIAL
01 · FIRST PEOPLES

Indigenous homelands

Long before written records, the Siuslaw and Lower Umpqua peoples lived from Heceta Head south through the river and dune country around present-day Reedsport. Around Coos Bay lived Hanis Coos and Miluk Coos peoples. Coastal communities drew food, materials, travel routes, and cultural knowledge from the ocean, estuaries, rivers, wetlands, and forests.

Colonization brought disease, dispossession, violence, and forced removal. Despite that disruption, descendants remain connected to these homelands. The Confederated Tribes of Coos, Lower Umpqua and Siuslaw Indians are a sovereign tribal nation whose members continue to steward culture and place.

THOUSANDS OF YEARS
02 · GEOLOGIC STORY

A landscape in motion

The dunes are the result of a long conversation among rivers, ocean currents, waves, wind, and vegetation. Chemical analysis points to the Umpqua River as the primary source of the sand, with contributions from the Siuslaw and smaller rivers.

Waves placed sand on the beach; prevailing winds carried dry grains inland. Over time, moving sand formed ridges, bowls, deflation plains, lakes, and long parabolic dunes reaching toward the forest. The shapes visitors see today are moments in a continuing process rather than permanent landforms.

MID-1800s–1900s
03 · WORKING COAST

Ports, roads, and industry

Euro-American settlement transformed the central coast. Florence, Gardiner, Reedsport, North Bend, and Coos Bay grew around river transport, fishing, lumber, shipbuilding, and trade. Jetties and navigation projects altered river mouths, while logging and roads connected coast communities to inland markets.

The same wind-driven sand that created the landscape also buried roads, threatened buildings, and complicated transportation. Communities increasingly treated moving dunes as a problem to be engineered.

EARLY–MID 1900s
04 · CHANGING THE DUNES

The effort to stop sand

Land managers planted European beachgrass to trap blowing sand and build a stable foredune near the beach. Later plantings and tree cover further slowed movement in many places. The work protected some roads and property, but it also interrupted the natural supply of sand moving inland.

Over decades, invasive plants converted broad open-sand habitat into dense vegetation. That unintended transformation became one of the defining conservation challenges of the modern Oregon Dunes.

1957
05 · FROM FLORENCE TO ARRAKIS

Frank Herbert and the road to Dune

Journalist Frank Herbert came to Florence in 1957 to research attempts to control shifting sand with vegetation. The magazine article he planned was never published, but the encounter sent him into years of research on ecology, power, resources, and the consequences of transforming a landscape.

Oregon’s coastal dunes helped spark the ecological questions that grew into one of science fiction’s most influential worlds.

Dune appeared in book form in 1965. Arrakis was not a copy of Oregon, but Herbert’s fascination with moving sand and large-scale ecological intervention began here.

POSTWAR–1970s
06 · A NEW KIND OF VISITOR

Recreation takes shape

After World War II, better highways, four-wheel-drive vehicles, camping trailers, motorcycles, and purpose-built off-road machines made the dunes accessible to more people. Families came to camp, fish, hike, explore lakes, and ride open sand.

As use increased, so did the need to separate activities, protect sensitive habitat, provide campgrounds, and define routes and riding zones. The modern patchwork of open riding areas, nonmotorized landscapes, developed sites, and habitat closures grew from those competing needs.

MARCH 23, 1972
07 · A NATIONAL RECREATION AREA

Congress draws a boundary

Congress established the Oregon Dunes National Recreation Area in 1972. The law directed the Forest Service to provide outdoor recreation while conserving the scenic, scientific, historic, and other values of the coast’s shorelines, dunes, forests, lakes, and facilities.

The designation protected 32,186 acres within the larger dune complex and formalized a balancing act that continues today: public access, local livelihoods, motorized and nonmotorized recreation, habitat, and a dynamic landscape all sharing one narrow coast.

1990s–TODAY
08 · THE NEXT CHAPTER

Restoring open dunes

Habitat work since the 1990s has included restoring open sand for the threatened western snowy plover and addressing invasive vegetation. Managers and community partners continue testing ways to recover dune movement without losing the access, infrastructure, and habitat people now depend on.

The modern story is not simply preservation versus use. It is a continuing negotiation among natural processes, tribal sovereignty, recreation, conservation science, working communities, and the consequences of earlier decisions.
